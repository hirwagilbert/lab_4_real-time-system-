# Report: scheduling rule, results and which stream suffered most

The gateway services four streams from one loop: temperature (period 1 s), humidity (0.5 s), air quality (2 s) and, in the heavier load, vibration (0.1 s). The loop is non-preemptive and serves exactly one due stream per pass. The documented rules are fixed priority with the shortest period first (FP, rate-monotonic), earliest deadline first (EDF) with the deadline set to the latest on-time start, and round-robin (RR). Each read takes a random processing time, with a 5 % chance of a long overrun. A sample is on-time if it starts within 10 % of its period of the required release, late if it starts afterwards but before the next release, and missed if the next release arrives first.

This follows the timing-budget idea of Section 4.1: every stream has its own acquisition requirement, and the shared loop must fit all reads, including their tails, into those periods. Nominal utilisation is 0.16 with three streams and 0.41 with four, but the measured busy fraction is 0.22 and 0.51 because of the overruns.

Averaged over 20 seeds, with three streams FP gave on-time shares of 94.1 % (temperature), 98.3 % (humidity) and 90.6 % (air quality), while RR gave 60.6 %, 70.5 % and 100.0 %. RR treats every due stream alike and ignores the periods, so a stream with a tight tolerance waits for a whole rotation. Adding the fast vibration stream lowered every stream under FP to 76 to 88 %, and vibration alone lost 7.6 % of its samples as missed. Under RR with four streams humidity fell to 54.7 % on-time, the worst result for a slow stream, and vibration lost 11.0 %. EDF was close to FP and slightly better for the slow streams (86.4, 88.8 and 83.9 %), with 8.9 % of vibration samples missed.

The stream that suffered most is therefore the vibration stream, because its tolerance of 10 ms is shorter than a single read of any other stream, and non-preemption means it must wait for that read to finish. Section 4.2 describes the same effect on a CAN bus: arbitration serves the highest-priority message first, yet a frame already in transmission cannot be interrupted, so worst-case latency has a blocking term. Among the slow streams, air quality has the longest reads, so its tail blocks the others and its own lateness is largest, but its 200 ms tolerance keeps it on-time more often.

Alerts were detected for every true crossing of the slow streams. Alert latency (0.09 s for the 0.1 s stream up to about 1.5 s for the 2 s stream) is dominated by the sampling period, since a crossing is seen only at the next sample, so a shorter period matters more than the scheduling rule for alerting. Under FP and RR one vibration crossing out of 40 was missed in seed 7.
